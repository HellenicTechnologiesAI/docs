Clear escalation paths prevent issues from falling through the cracks. At Hellenic Technologies, escalation is not a sign of failure — it is a professional obligation. Use this guide to determine when to escalate, who to escalate to, and how to do it in a way that keeps the client informed and moves toward resolution quickly.

---

## Escalation Triggers

Escalate immediately whenever any of the following conditions apply. Do not attempt to manage these situations alone.

- **P1 or P2 incident** — see the Support Tiers guide for classification criteria
- **Client threatening to terminate a contract** — even if you believe you can resolve the underlying issue
- **Repeated missed deliverables or timeline slippage** on an active project or retainer
- **Budget dispute or billing disagreement** — do not negotiate contract terms without Finance/Legal involvement
- **Legal or compliance concern** — including anything touching GDPR, intellectual property, contractual obligations, or potential litigation
- **Team capacity issues** that are threatening delivery quality — flag early, not after the deadline has been missed

When in doubt, escalate. It is far easier to stand down an escalation that turns out to be unnecessary than to recover from one that was delayed.

## Escalation Matrix

Use the matrix below to identify the right escalation path for each issue type.

| Issue Type | First Escalation | Second Escalation |
|---|---|---|
| Technical P1 / P2 | Technical Team Lead | CTO / Head of Development |
| Campaign performance issue | Senior Paid Media Specialist | Head of Digital Marketing |
| Client dissatisfaction / relationship at risk | Senior Account Manager | Client Services Director |
| Billing / contract dispute | Account Manager | Finance / Legal |
| GDPR / security incident | DPO | CEO |
| Delivery / timeline slippage | Project Manager / Team Lead | Client Services Director |
| Team capacity issue | Team Lead | Department Head |

If the first escalation point is unavailable (out of office, unresponsive), move directly to the second escalation point. Do not wait.

## Escalation Protocol

<Steps>
  <Step title="Document the Issue Clearly">
    Before you escalate, write a brief summary: what happened, when it happened, what the client impact is, and what you have already done to address it. The person you escalate to should be fully briefed before they speak to the client.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Notify the Escalation Point">
    For **P1/P2 incidents**: call the escalation contact directly — do not rely on Slack or email. For **P3/P4 or non-urgent issues**: escalate via Slack or email with a clear subject line flagging that it is an escalation.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Inform the Client">
    Do not leave the client in the dark. Let them know you are escalating, who will be taking ownership, and when they can expect the next update. Keep this message factual and calm — do not speculate about causes or timelines you are not certain of.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Maintain Regular Client Updates">
    For P1/P2 issues, the client must receive a status update at least every hour until the issue is resolved or a clear resolution path is confirmed. Assign one person to own client communication — do not have multiple team members messaging the client simultaneously.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Log and Close">
    Once the issue is resolved, log the full incident timeline in the CRM or project management tool. For P1/P2 incidents, schedule a brief post-mortem within 48 hours to identify the root cause and prevent recurrence.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## What Good Escalation Looks Like

A well-handled escalation leaves the client feeling that:

1. Their issue was taken seriously immediately
2. The right people were involved quickly
3. They were kept informed throughout
4. The team owned the problem and resolved it, rather than passing blame

A poorly handled escalation leaves the client feeling confused, ignored, or bounced between people with no clear owner.

---

<Tip>
When a situation is tense, remember that de-escalation starts with tone. Thank the client for their patience, acknowledge the issue directly ("I understand this has had a real impact on your business"), and focus the conversation on resolution — not on explaining why it happened or who is responsible. Clients want the problem fixed; save the root cause conversation for after the issue is resolved.
</Tip>
